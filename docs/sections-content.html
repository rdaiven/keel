Steps

A process, in order. Intro on one side, the numbered list on the other.

How a project runs

Four stages, in order, every time. Most projects take eight to twelve weeks end to end.

Ask about timing
  1. Listen. A long first conversation about what the site is for and who it serves.
  2. Draft. Real pages with real words — no mockups of pages that do not exist yet.
  3. Build. The draft becomes the site. You watch it happen and change your mind freely.
  4. Hand over. Keys, files, a walkthrough, and a quiet exit.

Native details/summary in a narrow container — no JavaScript, and every answer is findable in-page.

Questions we actually get

Do you work with clients outside the harbor?

Yes — about half our projects run entirely over email and calls. The pace is the same either way.

Can you take over a site someone else built?

Usually. We start with a paid one-day review so you know what you have before deciding anything.

Why only four projects a year?

Because each one gets a whole season. That is the pace the quality depends on, so we protect it.

What if I just need a small change?

Past clients: email us, it is probably covered by care. New to us: small changes are how we usually meet people.

Long-form prose beside a captioned figure. The wide-start split gives the words the bigger column.

Why we write before we design

Every project here starts as a page of plain words: what the site is for, who it serves, what it should say first. There is no layout yet, and nothing to hide behind — if a sentence is vague on that page, it will be vague on the finished site too.

Only when the words hold do we open the design files. It feels slow for the first two weeks and fast for every week after, because nobody is designing around a gap.

If the words stand up without the design, the design has something to stand on.

Accordion

Long-form content folded into one surface. Native details grouped by name, so opening one closes the rest — no JavaScript.

The longer answers

Process notes that run long, folded so the page stays short. The group shares a name, so one opens at a time.

Why the studio stays at two people

Every time we have been tempted to hire, the reason was a backlog — and a backlog is a scheduling problem, not a staffing one. Four projects a year fit two people exactly, with room left to answer old clients the week they write.

Staying small also keeps the promise simple: the person you meet in the first call is the person who builds the site and the person who answers three years later.

What we mean by a site that holds

A site holds when nothing about it depends on us. The files are plain HTML and CSS, the hosting is in your name, and the maintenance guide fits on one page. If the studio vanished tomorrow, your site would not notice.

Thirty-eight of the forty-one sites we have shipped are still online. The three that are not were retired on purpose, by their owners, without needing our help.

How the seasonal calendar works

Each project gets a season: roughly twelve weeks with a beginning, a middle, and an agreed end. We book a season ahead and never double-book, which is why the answer to "can you start next week" is almost always no.

The trade is worth naming: you wait longer to start, and in exchange the project has our whole attention while it runs. Nobody here is context-switching between six sites.
